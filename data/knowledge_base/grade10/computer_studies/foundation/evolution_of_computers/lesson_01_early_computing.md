## 1. Introduction

For thousands of years, humans have needed ways to **count, calculate, record, and process information**.

In ancient communities, people needed to keep track of things such as cattle, harvested crops, trade, and time. At first, people relied on their fingers, stones, and marks made on sticks.

As societies grew and calculations became more complicated, these methods became increasingly difficult to manage accurately. This led to the development of tools that could make calculations faster and more reliable.

Early computing devices did not look like today's computers and did not use electricity. However, they introduced important ideas that contributed to modern computing, including:

- Representing information physically
- Following systematic procedures
- Storing numerical values
- Automating calculations

## 2. The Abacus

The **abacus** is one of the oldest known calculating devices. It was developed in ancient times and became highly developed in China, where a form of it is known as the **Suanpan**.

### Physical Description

An abacus consists of:

- A rectangular frame
- Parallel rods
- Movable beads
- A horizontal beam separating the beads into sections

A commonly used Chinese abacus has:

### Upper Deck — Heaven

The upper deck usually contains one or two beads per rod. Each bead represents a value of **5**.

### Lower Deck — Earth

The lower deck usually contains four or five beads per rod. Each bead represents a value of **1**.

The rods represent place values, moving from right to left:

**Ones → Tens → Hundreds → Thousands → ...**

Values are represented by moving beads toward the central beam.

### Uses

The abacus can be used for:

- Addition
- Subtraction
- Multiplication
- Division

### Importance to Modern Computing

The abacus demonstrates the concept of **data representation**.

Physical objects—in this case, beads—can represent abstract numerical information.

The positions of the beads can also be viewed as storing a numerical state while the user performs other parts of a calculation.

## 3. Napier's Bones

**Napier's Bones** were developed by Scottish mathematician **John Napier** in 1617.

The device consisted of marked rods made from materials such as wood, bone, ivory, or heavy cardboard.

### How They Worked

The device used rods containing multiplication tables.

The rods were placed beside one another and the user read the appropriate values across the rows. Digits were then combined along diagonal sections to perform the required calculation.

### Uses

Napier's Bones helped with:

- **Multiplication**
- **Division**

They simplified complicated calculations by reducing them to a series of simpler operations.

### Importance to Modern Computing

Napier's Bones demonstrate the ideas of:

- **Lookup tables**
- **Breaking complex problems into simpler steps**
- **Algorithmic procedures**

This is an important connection to modern computing because computer programs also break problems into a sequence of operations.

## 4. The Pascaline

The **Pascaline** was a mechanical calculator developed by French mathematician **Blaise Pascal** in 1642.

Pascal developed it partly to help his father, who worked as a tax collector and had to perform large numbers of calculations.

### Physical Description

The Pascaline consisted of:

- Gears
- Wheels
- Dials
- Input wheels marked from 0 to 9

The user entered numbers by turning the wheels.

### Automatic Carry Mechanism

One of its important features was its **automatic carry mechanism**.

For example, when the units wheel completed a rotation from 9 back to 0, the mechanism automatically moved the tens wheel forward by one position.

This is similar to what happens when we calculate:

**9 + 1 = 10**

The device mechanically handled the transition from the ones column to the tens column.

### Uses

The Pascaline could perform:

- Addition
- Subtraction

### Importance to Modern Computing

The Pascaline demonstrated that some calculation processes could be **mechanised**.

Instead of a person manually performing every step, gears could perform part of the calculation automatically.

## 5. The Slide Rule

The **slide rule** is an analog computing device developed from logarithmic principles.

It was invented in the 1620s and became an important tool for scientists and engineers.

### Main Parts

A slide rule contains:

1. **Stock** — the outer body containing mathematical scales.
2. **Slide** — the central section that moves left and right.
3. **Cursor** — a transparent indicator used to align values.

### How It Works

Instead of representing numbers using individual beads or gear positions, a slide rule uses **logarithmic scales**.

The physical distance between values represents mathematical relationships.

### Uses

Slide rules could be used for:

- Multiplication
- Division
- Squares
- Square roots
- Cubes
- Exponents
- Trigonometric calculations

### Importance to Modern Computing

The slide rule is an example of **analog computing** because it represents information using continuous physical quantities such as distance.

It was widely used by engineers and scientists before electronic calculators became common.

## 6. Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Computing device** | A tool or machine designed to assist with calculation, storing values, or processing data. |
| **Mechanical calculator** | A calculating device that uses physical moving parts such as gears and wheels. |
| **Calculation** | The process of using mathematical operations to produce a result. |
| **Algorithm** | A precise sequence of steps used to solve a problem or complete a task. |
| **Analog device** | A device that represents or processes information using continuous physical quantities. |
| **Place value** | The value represented by a digit because of its position in a number. |
| **Automatic carry** | A mechanism that moves a value to the next numerical position when a lower position reaches its limit. |

## 7. Kenyan and Everyday Examples

### Abacus and Market Trading

Imagine a market trader using stones or bottle tops to keep track of quantities.

The objects can represent numbers in different place-value positions.

This demonstrates the basic principle behind an abacus.

### Motorcycle Odometer

When a motorcycle's odometer changes from:

**0099 → 0100**

one numerical position rolls over and causes the next position to advance.

This provides an everyday illustration of the idea behind the Pascaline's automatic carry mechanism.

### Posho Mill

A mechanical machine takes an input, performs a sequence of physical operations, and produces an output.

This can help learners understand the broader idea of:

**Input → Processing → Output**

### Measuring Tape

A measuring tape demonstrates how physical distance can be used to obtain information, providing an accessible analogy for the continuous measurement principle behind analog devices.

## 10. Common Misconceptions

### Misconception 1

**"The abacus was an electronic computer."**

**Correction:** The abacus is a manual calculating device. It does not contain electronic components.

### Misconception 2

**"Early computing devices had nothing to do with modern computers."**

**Correction:** Early devices introduced important ideas such as representing information, performing systematic calculations, and automating operations.

### Misconception 3

**"Computers were invented suddenly."**

**Correction:** Modern computing developed gradually through many devices and technological advances.

## 11. Summary

Early computing devices developed because humans needed better ways to calculate and manage information.

The major devices studied in this lesson are:

- **Abacus** — represented numbers using beads and place values.
- **Napier's Bones** — simplified multiplication and division using prepared numerical tables.
- **Pascaline** — used mechanical gears to automate arithmetic.
- **Slide Rule** — used logarithmic scales for mathematical calculations.

Together, these devices demonstrate important concepts that remain relevant to computing today:

**Data representation → Algorithms → Storage → Calculation → Automation**

## 12. Revision Notes

### The Problem

Human memory and manual calculation have limitations.

### Evolution

**Manual tools → Calculation aids → Mechanical calculators → Analog computing devices**

### Important Concepts

- **Data representation:** Physical objects can represent information.
- **Automation:** A machine can perform some operations automatically.
- **Algorithm:** A problem can be solved through a sequence of steps.
- **Analog vs digital:** Information can be represented using continuous quantities or distinct values.
